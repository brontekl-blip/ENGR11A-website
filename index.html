Design Project #1

Part 1: Customized Name Tag

We started class by downloading and setting up prusaslicer, and then played with settings for a bit before slicing it and downloading the G code to a USB drive and bringing it to the printers. We used the Prusa MINIs with Input Shapers and PLA, which are the same machines and filament I used in all prints for this project. I purposefully chose a funky font, “Old English,” to see how well the printer would do, and it did mess up a bit, with the smaller details of the letters being lost. Figuring out how to load the filament was a little tricky, and we changed colors part of the way through so I had to unload and reload the filament. Overall, I think the print was pretty successful.

Part 2: The Benchy Torture Test

It took a few minutes to download the right file so that I could edit the type of filament and printer. It seems like after looking into it a bit, .stl files are less customizable and store less information about the intended end result of the print compared to .mpf files that can contain things like intended colors. I started the print at the end of a class and picked it up off the printer at the beginning of the next, and it seems to have printed without any major flaws/errors. In the pictures below you can see some “stringing” when transitioning from one area of the print to another, the minor flaws in the finer details, as well as the less-sleak aligned seams on the two cylinders of the build. This is a good demonstration of the limits of 3D printing objects without harsh edges, and how you might need to use a different seam technique, such as randomized, depending on the geometry of what you print. The arches and pot windows seem to have had the hangovers created at enough of an angle to maintain their desired shapes without dripping.

Part 3: Open Source Assistive Devices

The 3D printable assistive device I found was Flipper the Clipper, a one-handed tool to simplify the use of nail clippers for people who may struggle with grip strength, hand steadiness, etc. The designer was nisker_ on Thingiverse, who adapted it from designer SONOKO’s original idea but to have an integrated hinge and be easier to 3D print. It was a single print that took about an hour, which was less than the 3.5 hours predicted by the listing. A design flaw I noticed while trying to use it was that the hinge area is too narrow for most nail clippers, as can be seen in the first few photos of the printed version below, and should probably be adjusted to accommodate a wider variety of sizing. After taking another look, the post on Thingiverse recommends using Onshape to customize the design to fit your own nail clippers.

Part 4: Personal Website

The final part of our assignment was designing a website to upload our class projects to, using BootStrap and GitHub. We started by watching tutorials on the basics of coding a website with BootStrap, such as common coding language and boundaries. We then spent some time in class for troubleshooting, but most of the website building was done outside of class in my case. Since we had templates, it was relatively easy to just replace the images and texts with my own, but sizing and spacing is still a struggle as you might be able to tell from the initial iteration of this project’s upload (hopefully I will be able to go back and fix this later down the line).

Design Project #2

Part 1: Design Research and Planning

I found a pencil cup and post-it holder, and merged them for a single stationary holder. In the Engineering Classroom, we use post-it notes very often to label our printing jobs with their assigned numbers, so through this design I combined where one would get both the post-it and pen to label their print job, reducing mess and making a repeated action more automatic. This utilizes the Systematic Inventive Thinking pattern “task unification.”

To address the constraints of this project: Both a pencil cup and post-it holder are small everyday objects, so it would be plausible to print them together on a PrusaMINI. Neither should require supports as they should sit flat on a table and do not require dangling/overhanging pieces. The object can be printed in PLA, and is not an object that should be in contact with food, and also I do not anticipate it being in high-heat locations. Additionally, it holds post-it notes and pens/pencils, which are everyday objects that are used regularly by many people.

Part 2: Tinkercad Remix

I kind of just found a fun pencil cup and a standard post-it note holder and shoved them together in Tinkercad. I made the pencil cup a lot smaller to fit on the Prusa MINI+, so I lost some of the details and the shoes ended up with holes in some spots as the wall was most likely too thin to hold, but overall it looked decent.

It fit probably 5-6 writing instruments and several post-it pads stacked. I think if I had more expertise and experience on CAD I might have been able to fix the shoes, but as it stands it is not a function-breaking flaw that I felt was necessary to fix for my next version. If I had more time I might have attempted it. I think the cup itself being smaller works well as it doesn’t take up too much desk space but allows for a small variety of pens and pencils.

Part 3: Onshape Remix

I wanted to use the sketch and parametric solid modelling tools to add another feature to my design, and something that I thought would be easy to add and model as a beginner, as well as useful, was a phone stand. The back wall of the phone stand would be a bit higher than half the phone to prop it up, and the front wall would be just high enough to secure the bottom of the phone at an angle while still leaving the phone screen accessible. I measured my phone’s dimensions, as well as the distance from the point of contact with the phone and the most distal point of the bottom of my phone when leaned at a good angle against an object – this was to ensure that the phone stand had enough room for the phone to lean securely against the phone stand instead of being at a right angle from the table, as that would make the phone more likely to fall forward since the front wall of the phone stand would be too low to prevent that.

In my first attempt to print, I realized after the first layer that the phone stand was printing as a separate object from the rest of the print, despite them being right next to each other, so I had to stop the print. I went back into Onshape and just pushed the phone stand slightly into the wall of the post-it stand, and when I reprinted it they printed connected.

For the final 3D print product, there was a strange flaw where the edges of the phone stand were not flat, but had a sort of serrated edge, as seen in some of the pictures below. I also forgot to change the settings for random seams, so the seam after the post-it holder ended was unfortunately placed along the pencil cup where the face was. I think I achieved the goal of creating an additional feature for my existing design. Something that I could optimize would be a way to charge the phone while it is in the stand. The bottom of the stand would have to be thicker to accommodate space for the charger and cable to comfortably go under the phone.

Admittedly I did not use Tinkercad very much other than to combine the original two objects for my first print. It felt like a bit more of a simple program to use; I wasn’t sure how easy it would be to create something new in an exact and detailed manner, but this might be because I do not know the tools as well. Onshape seemed more capable of complex and customized objects, however because of all the tools it can be harder to navigate. I also had a hard time with the controls for moving your perspective, and defaulted to just using the square control in the corner to change my view. Additionally, in general it can be hard to tell whether two objects are touching enough to print as one thing.

Design Project #3

Part 1: Robot Assembly

For this project, I worked with Joyce Li. My partner and I each built one of the robots. I think the manual would benefit from specifying when to solder parts into place, as it was sometimes unclear. For example, when installing the motor and wires. It was also difficult to take inventory of all the parts, as they were only listed by name, and I didn’t know the names for various different parts.

This was my first time soldering, but surprisingly it was not as difficult as I had expected. I still struggled with some spots that were harder to reach when working around previously soldered spots, like the LEDs on the bottom. The only part of assembly that I didn’t do myself was putting the gaskets on the screws, since I couldn’t figure out how to do it and my partner knew how.

Part 2: Design and 3D Print a Carbox

V1 of our 3D-printed car box was designed by Joyce to fit tightly on the robot, however it did not account for the space needed for the wires coming out of the battery case. The design was also not narrow enough where the top LEDs were, and so could not fit the front-end of the robot. The last issue was that the shaft that led to the on/off button was very narrow and did not fit well around the button itself.

V2 of our 3d-printed car box was designed by me as a straight-forward box that was a loose fit. I used the photo from the manual as a guide, but made the inner walls larger than the measurements of the robot to make sure it wouldn’t be too small on the robot. The hole to reach the on/off button was made large enough to fit a human finger. The main issues with this design were that it was too loose, as there was no siding that extended below the edges of the PCB, and the cutouts for the top LEDs were too short to fit on my robot. Additionally, which I only realized later on, the wheel cutouts were different sizes on each side.

V3 of our 3D-printed car box was also designed by me, and was mostly a few adaptations from V2. I standardized the sizes of the wheel cutouts, extended the LED cutouts upwards by 2mm, and added siding that extended 3mm downward along each side to keep the car box better in place. This final version fit well, so I am very happy with our results.

Part 3: Design and Laser Cut a Carbox

For this portion of the project, Joyce mainly made the versions, and I made one just to make sure I had the skill of turning a 3D print design into a laser cut design. Due to my absence during two classes for this project, I did not have the time to complete a practice laser cut.

Joyce’s V1 started with a simple box very similar to 3D print V2 but with the siding that extended below the PCB to keep it in place, which turned out well, except we forgot to add wheel cutouts. In V2 she added the wheel cutouts, which was a sufficient model. She then made V3 to be more aesthetic and look like a real car, with a small hole in the back to reach the on/off button.

My laser cut design was the exact same as my 3D print V3 design, but with separate parts and finger joints that allow it to be laid out for laser cutting. It functioned just as well as the 3D print version.

Part 2: Printing and Assembling the Prosthesis

I printed the finger joint parts, swivel pin, and thumb tensioner pin, and assembled a majority of the hand. Gerardo printed the remaining hand parts and did the final stringing fixation. We could not find the right size screws, so the gripper box and associated parts were glued together instead. Even the demo bar’s screws do not quite fit that well, but it is fixed well enough.

The grip is not the best, as the stringing is a bit loose and the pointer finger’s line does not go through the knuckle joint. As shown in the final 2 pictures, the thumb joint broke on the knuckle, so it is a bit limp. The velcro strap also doesn’t fully secure the gauntlet to your arm, so the wrist-driven mechanics aren’t the best unless you hold it in place to your arm with your other hand.

Part 4: Task Specfic Modification

We decided on an accessory that attaches to the fingers to assist in holding your phone. This uses the task unification principle. We had initially planned something that clips onto the fingers, but due to complexity and time constraints we went with a design that slides in between the finger joints. It is not fully secured to the hand, so it would be possible for it to slip out if the fingers and tilted too far towards the ground, but overall it works for long-term phone usage. We also added a foam piece at the top of the clip so the phone fits snuggly.

Part 5: Reflection, Contribution, and Bridging to Capstone

To relate this to the Capstone project, the grip on this hand does not lend itself well to container-opening. A product that allows for easier container opening, such as cans and jars, would be useful for someone with an upper limb difference.
